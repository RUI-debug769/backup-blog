For the past few days, I have been at Baima Lake in Hangzhou, working with two other people on something called Diting. The name comes from the mythical beast that crouches on the ground and listens; what we borrowed is the act of "listening," not its appearance.

## 1. What It Is

In one sentence: a small device that sits on your desk and listens only to you. It records silently during the day, and when you want to look, it compresses the day into three lines.

It is not a diary. A diary requires you to sit down and write, but the hardest part of keeping records has never been the writing—it is that **nobody looks at them afterward**. So it takes a different approach: instead of giving you a more complete manuscript, it gives you something **shorter and more worth reading right now**.

## 2. Why "Recording"

Recording has never been objective. The court historians who wrote the emperor's daily records would not even let the emperor read them. Today everyone is being recorded, but the things recording you are recommendation algorithms and shopping carts.

So this project is not about "inventing a more accurate voice recorder." Diary apps have huge download numbers but extremely low seven-day retention, which shows that the demand has always been there and the friction is simply too high. People have always consumed podcasts and oral histories, which shows that we love hearing how others tell the story of their lives. The essence of this demand is not "write it down," but "be understood."

Diting tries to turn that around a little: to let something that listens only to you, can be contradicted by you, and can make mistakes remember how you lived.

## 3. Three Design Decisions

**The face does not belong on the hardware.** The hardware is a T5AI-CORE board (73×29mm), and for now it does only one thing: capture audio and send it to the server. The original plan also included a breathing light with five states—standby, listening, hearing someone else, being contradicted, and writing at night. The light has not been built yet, so this version remains silent. A screen would pull the work back toward consumer electronics, while a breathing light feels more like a living thing—I still believe that; it just has not had its turn yet.

**No verbatim transcript, just three columns of judgments.** A day contains two to four hundred utterances, but fewer than ten are truly worth revisiting. The problem with a transcript is that it leaves the organizing work to you. The current approach is this: after the model reads everything said that day, it outputs only three things—**facts** (what you told it), **to-dos** (what remains undone), and **needs your attention** (contradictory statements, promises made to others, last-minute changes of mind, and things mentioned repeatedly without action). The headline reports only one number: how many things need your attention today. Facts do not count, because facts are the underlying draft, not something requiring action from you.

Compression ratio is the core metric of this project. The three columns together usually contain fewer than one hundred Chinese characters, while the original speech runs to one or two thousand—twentyfold to thirtyfold compression is the prerequisite for "no time needed to read it."

**I abandoned the "refutable" approach.** The original idea was to let every sentence be flagged with "I did not say that," have the system remember the denial, and phrase it differently next time. Only after building it did I realize that an interface requiring you to judge every sentence is more exhausting than a transcript. It has now been replaced by something stricter—**other people's words never enter the ledger at all**. It hears everyone, but only registered voices are recorded. A judge can stand in front of it and speak, and the left column does not move; that single moment explains what it is better than any button could.

## 4. What the Three Columns Look Like

The first thing you see is a sentence: "There are 5 things for you to review today."

Below it are three cards: needs your attention (with a coral label), to-dos, and facts. The original utterances sit underneath, collapsed by default and expanded only when tapped—**only the most recent 7 days are kept, and older material is automatically deleted**. Forgetting is a feature, not a defect.

The counter was the most reworked part of this version. The old algorithm was simply "number of to-dos + number of attention items," which meant the same thing phrased two different ways was counted twice; "return the book to the library" appearing in both to-dos and attention items was counted as two as well. Now the text is normalized first (punctuation removed and full-width/half-width forms unified), then merged according to three rules: identical after normalization, one longer sentence containing another, or more than sixty percent overlap in two-character groups all count as the same item. If a fact collides with a to-do, it is deleted outright—the value of the three columns lies in division of labor, not repetition.

Writing is streamed. The model can finish in a second, but filling the screen all at once leaves no time to read, so everything is received first and then released at one character every 30 milliseconds, with a cursor at the end of the item currently being written. It automatically speeds up when it falls too far behind, so no matter how fast the model is, it will never get more than half a second ahead of you. While it writes, the item count in the headline is frozen; only after it finishes does the new number appear—otherwise it would jump from "5 items" to "nothing for you to review" and then jump back.

## 5. A Stone Beast That Watches What You Are Doing

A pixel-art stone beast stands on the screen, along the upper edge of "let it write."

It is not there to be cute. All nine frames correspond to real states in the pipeline: you say something and it is recorded, and its ears perk up; the speaker is not the owner, and it closes its eyes and shakes its head; tap "let it write," and it stares at the coral dot appearing in the upper-right corner while it thinks; the model begins producing text, and its eyes droop while the tassel on top sways from side to side; when the writing is done, its eyes curve into two smiles; when the pendant disconnects, its whole body slumps; after three minutes with no attention, it falls asleep with zzz floating above its head.

My first attempt was a blinking blocky cat, too ugly to look at. The second version became an ink-wash-like lump of stone, which was too abstract. In the end I returned to pixels: a 20×18 grid, filled in one square at a time, where changing an expression means changing only a few lines of strings. The virtue of pixel art is that it is inherently cheap and inherently honest—you can tell at a glance that it is drawn, and you will not mistake it for a real pet.

## 6. Looking Back

The date at the top of the main screen can be tapped to open a monthly calendar. Days with records have a small coral dot underneath; tap one to see that day's three columns and original utterances.

Past days are **read-only, never rewritten**—you cannot let today's model rewrite the day before yesterday's judgments; that would become something very strange. There is only one way back: "Return to today."

Two small icons sit in the same area: one switches between dark and light themes, where dark is warm black and light is warm paper; the other opens settings. Every button on the settings page has been replaced with a thin-line icon and a short label—this project does not compile `res/` or generate `R`, so the icons are also drawn stroke by stroke with `Path`.

## 7. Turning One Character into an Icon

The icon went through three versions, and every version failed in the same place.

The first version copied the beast's form from a figurine—large ears, antlers, and a bell on its collar. It looked too much like a mascot, and the more detailed it became, the more it looked like a sticker.

The second version abandoned the beast and drew only "listening": a dozen or so brushstrokes converging from the outside inward toward a vermilion center. The direction was right, but the length and thickness of the strokes wavered, and at 48px it blurred into a blob.

The third version restored order: the ring was given more weight, nine strokes were connected directly to it, the vermilion dot was enlarged, and a ring of paper white was left between the stroke tips and the dot. The self-referential idea survived—the ring travels an extra 50°, folds inside its own starting point, and tapers at the end, so the border enters the content. This step is visible at 512px and disappears automatically at 48px, which is exactly right.

Three hard constraints came up while making the Android adaptive icon, and I am recording them here:

- **VectorDrawable does not support SVG filters.** Hand-drawn jitter must be baked into the path coordinates; you cannot rely on `feTurbulence`;
- **The 72dp safe area.** On the 108dp canvas, the ink ring converts to 35.02dp, just inside the 36dp-radius circle; any larger and the circular mask would clip it;
- **The ring's tail cannot have a "stem."** Any thick stroke that leaves the ring and crosses the canvas will be read as a flower stem, instantly turning the whole image into "flower + stalk."

## 8. Where the Sound Goes

This version needs to state this clearly, because I got it wrong in writing once before.

The microphone on the board sends sound to **a server of our own**, where it is segmented, attributed to speakers, and transcribed; the audio is discarded as soon as transcription finishes—it is never written to disk on the server, nor does it enter a database. The phone and server communicate over an unencrypted, plaintext TCP connection. So the accurate claim is not "the sound never leaves the premises," but rather: **the sound goes only to our own server, is discarded upon arrival, and only text remains.**

Recognition is multilingual: Chinese, English, Japanese, Korean, and Cantonese are all detected automatically, with no manual selection required. Speaker recognition uses CAM++ voiceprints, and the threshold is made stricter for short single-sentence utterances to avoid identifying the wrong person from half a sentence. Both capabilities are still merely "adequate," not "good"—heavy dialects, continuous speech, and background music all cause failures.

## 9. Current Status

What works: the full board → server → phone pipeline runs on real hardware; three-column judgments, streamed writing, calendar history, and both dark and light themes; voices other than the owner's do not enter the ledger; and the desktop pet follows the system state.

What does not work, listed item by item, because this section matters more than every section before it—

- It does not know what you are thinking, only what you said;
- It cannot distinguish "you truly think this" from "you said it in passing";
- It cannot remember your tone, only your words;
- Your friends will not like it, because it does not listen to them;
- What it writes will be wrong, and wrong with great confidence;
- The hardware is not yet wearable: a development board plus a power bank, with three mountains still between it and a "pendant"—an enclosure, power consumption, and offline caching.

There is also one question I have no answer for: **do people actually want to see "another version of themselves"?** What most people want to see may simply be "a better version of themselves." If users open it hoping to see a self that has been understood and idealized, then this kind of biased retelling will make them uncomfortable. That will determine whether this product retains people or hurts them.
